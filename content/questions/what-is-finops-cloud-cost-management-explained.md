A five-figure cloud bill lands on someone's desk, engineering blames usage spikes, finance blames lack of visibility, and nobody can explain where the money actually went. This is exactly the scenario FinOps exists to prevent, and it's a common interview topic because it tests whether you think about infrastructure as a cost center or as a business investment.

## The answer interviewers want

FinOps is getting engineering and finance to speak the same language about cloud costs. Instead of surprise bills showing up at month-end, teams get real-time visibility into spending as it happens, not after the invoice arrives.

The goal isn't just cutting costs. It's making sure every dollar spent drives business value. That means shifting the conversation from raw infrastructure spend to something like cost-per-feature: what did this capability actually cost to build and run, and is that justified by what it delivers.

The part most candidates miss is that FinOps isn't about saying no to everything. A FinOps-mature team isn't the one that blocks every provisioning request to save money. It's about saying yes to the right things with full cost awareness, so spending decisions are made deliberately instead of by default.

## Why interviewers ask this

This question screens for whether you treat cost as an afterthought or as a first-class engineering concern. Interviewers want to see that you understand FinOps as a cross-functional practice, not a finance-only process or a cost-cutting mandate bolted onto DevOps. They're also checking whether you can connect technical decisions to business outcomes, which matters more as engineers move into senior and platform roles.

## Follow-ups to expect

**How do you give engineering real-time cost visibility instead of waiting for month-end bills?** Point to the shift the source implies: moving from after-the-fact invoices to ongoing, in-the-loop visibility so teams see spend as it accrues rather than as a surprise.

**What does cost-per-feature actually measure, and why is it more useful than total infrastructure spend?** It ties a number to the value delivered rather than just the resources consumed, which lets teams judge whether a feature's cost is justified instead of just whether the bill went up.

**How do you avoid FinOps turning into engineering being told no to everything?** Frame it as enabling informed yes decisions: give teams cost data up front so they can choose the right option deliberately, rather than having spend vetoed after the fact.

## The common wrong answer

Most candidates describe FinOps as cost-cutting: tagging resources, killing idle instances, and shrinking the bill. That's not wrong as a tactic, but it misses the actual point. The source is clear that cutting costs isn't the goal, value per dollar spent is. Candidates who stop at "it's about reducing cloud spend" miss the business-value and collaboration angle that interviewers are actually listening for.
