This isn't really a technical question, but it shows up in behavioral rounds and in recruiter screens, because companies want to know if you'll negotiate hard or just take the first number. How you handle it signals whether you understand your own value in the market, which is a proxy for how you'll advocate for your team's resourcing later.

## The answer interviewers want

Start with research, not a gut feeling. Pull numbers from `levels.fyi` and Glassdoor, and ask people in your network who hold similar titles at similar-sized companies. Get at least three data points before you say a number out loud. One data point is an anecdote, three is a range you can defend.

Next, quantify your impact instead of listing responsibilities. "I worked on CI/CD" is forgettable. "I reduced deployment time by 40%" or "I saved $200K in cloud costs" is not. Write these numbers down before the conversation so you're not fishing for them live.

The part most people miss: negotiate total compensation, not just base salary. Stock, PTO, and learning or conference budget all have real value, and some of them are easier for a hiring manager to move than base pay, especially if base is capped by a band. If the recruiter won't budge on salary, that's often where the actual room is.

Put it together and the pitch isn't "pay me more," it's "here's my market range based on three sources, here's the measurable impact I've had, and here's how I'd like the total package structured." That's confident without being combative.

## Why interviewers ask this

This isn't about the number itself, it's about whether you can make a case with evidence instead of emotion. Recruiters and hiring managers are also quietly checking if you've done basic homework, since someone who hasn't researched comp for the role probably hasn't researched the company or the team either.

## Follow-ups to expect

- **"What if the recruiter won't move on base salary?"** Shift the conversation to the rest of the package, stock, PTO, signing bonus, learning budget, since those are often more flexible than the base band.
- **"How do you back up a number like that 40% deployment time reduction?"** Have the before-and-after measurement ready, even roughly, because a claim without a baseline sounds made up.
- **"What if your three data points disagree wildly?"** Treat the spread as a range tied to scope and seniority, and use it to justify where in the range you're asking to land, not to pick the highest number and hope.

## The common wrong answer

The common failure is anchoring on a single source, usually one Glassdoor number or one friend's offer, and repeating it with no supporting detail when pushed. The other failure is negotiating base salary in isolation and accepting whatever PTO, stock, or learning budget is offered by default, which is exactly where money gets left on the table.
